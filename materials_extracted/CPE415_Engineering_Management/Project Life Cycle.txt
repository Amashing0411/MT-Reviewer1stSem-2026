As Computer Engineers, you will rarely work on isolated tasks. You will be designing 
embedded systems, deploying enterprise networks, or developing complex software applications.

II.  Conceptual Foundations: The Systems Development Cycle (SDC) 
According to Nicholas & Steyn (2021), every human -made system—whether it is a new 
microarchitecture, a nationwide 5G network, or a robotic assembly line —follows a logical 
sequence of development called the Systems Development Cycle (SDC) . The SDC consists of 
four distinct phases: Conception, Definition, Execution, and Operation. The first three phases 
constitute the Project Life Cycle. 
Understanding this cycle is critical because decisions made in the early phases lock in the 
majority of the project's costs and determine its ultimate success or failure. 
Phase A: Conception (Project Initiation and Feasibility) 
The conception phase begins when a customer, user, or sponsor recognizes a need, a 
problem, or an opportunity. 
1. Initial Investigation:  This is a "quick and dirty" assessment. A need is identified (e.g., "Our 
university campus Wi -Fi is too slow and has dead zones"). The organization performs a 
preliminary study to determine if the problem is worth solving. If it aligns with organizational  
goals, it moves to the next step. 
2. Project Feasibility Study:  If the initial idea survives, a formal feasibility study is conducted. 
This study rigorously evaluates alternative solutions based on: 
● Technical Feasibility:  Does the technology exist to solve this? Do we possess the 
engineering talent? 
● Economic Feasibility: Will the benefits (ROI) outweigh the costs? 
● Operational Feasibility: Will the end-users actually adopt and use the system? 
3. The Request for Proposal (RFP) and Proposal Generation: If the customer lacks the internal 
resources to build the system, they outsource it. They publish an RFP, a formal document sent to 
potential contractors. A good RFP contains the Statement of Work (SOW), expected deliverables, 
timeline, and proposal evaluation criteria. Contractors review the RFP and, if they believe they can 
technically and profitably deliv er the system, they submit a Proposal. The proposal is a sales 
document and a preliminary project plan rolled into one. It outlines the contractor's technical 
approach, project management plan, and cost estimate. 
Phase B: Definition (System and Project Definition) 
Once a proposal is accepted and a contract is signed, the Definition phase begins. Nicholas 
& Steyn (2021) refer to this as "front-end loading." The goal here is to establish exactly what will 
be built and how the project will be managed. 
1. System Definition and Requirements Analysis:  This is where many tech projects fail. 
Customers speak in User Requirements (vague, operational needs like "The app must be secure

translate these into System Requirements (what the system must do to meet the user need) and 
finally into System Specifications (highly detailed, measurable, technical criteria). 
● User Requirement: "The drone must fly long enough to map a 50-acre farm."

To ensure no features are missed and no unnecessary features are added (preventing "scope 
creep"), engineers use a Requirements Traceability Matrix. This document maps every technical 
specification back to a specific user requirement. 
2. Project Definition:  Parallel to defining the system, the project manager defines the project. 
This involves creating the detailed execution plan, establishing the final budget, and setting the 
baseline schedule. 
Phase C: Execution (Acquisition) 
This is the longest and most capital -intensive phase of the project life cycle. The plans 
made in Phase B are put into action. 
1. Detail Design:  Engineers create schematics, PCB layouts, and software architectures. 
Prototypes are built and tested. 
2. Production/Build: The system is fabricated or coded. 
3. Implementation (System Conversion): The system is installed in the user's environment. 
For IT and CpE projects, Nicholas & Steyn identify three primary conversion strategies: 
● Parallel Installation: The old system and new system run simultaneously until the 
new system is proven. (High cost, low risk). 
● Pilot Operation: The new system is rolled out to a small fraction of users first. 
(Moderate cost, moderate risk). 
● Cold Turkey (Big Bang): The old system is turned off, and the new system is turned 
on immediately. (Low cost, extremely high risk). 
4. Closeout: The project is handed over to the user. A crucial, often overlooked step is the 
Post-Completion Project Review  (Postmortem), where the team documents "lessons 
learned" to improve future projects. 
Phase D: Operation 
The project is officially over. The system is now operational and in the hands of the end -
users. The original project team is disbanded, though a separate maintenance or support team may 
take over to handle upgrades and repairs until the system reaches the  end of its useful life and is 
disposed of.

III. Computer Engineering Contextualization: Waterfall vs. Agile in the SDC 
The standard four-phase SDC heavily implies a "Waterfall" methodology: you finish Phase 
B (Definition) completely before starting Phase C (Execution). For building a bridge or a 
skyscraper, this is mandatory. You cannot pour a concrete foundation while sti ll deciding how 
many floors the building will have. 
However, in software engineering and embedded systems design, requirements change 
rapidly. If a CpE team spends a year in Phase B defining software specifications, the technology 
or market may have changed by the time they start Phase C. 
Therefore, modern Engineering Management adapts the SDC using Rapid Prototyping 
and Iterative (Agile) Development. In this adapted model, the team moves quickly from a high -
level Phase B into a mini -Phase C to build a small, functional prototype. They show this to the 
user, gather feedback, and cycle back to Phase B to refine the requirements. This ensures the final 
system actually meets the user's needs, even if those needs evolve during the project.
